Still copying WhatsApp orders into Excel? Map your order flow first

Every order typed twice costs time, and the second copy is where the mistakes come from. Here is how to see the real cost, and what to fix first.

By Onyxx Tech25 November 20267 min read

Download as PDFFree. Save it, print it, share it with your team.

If your team copies orders from WhatsApp into Excel by hand, you are paying for every order twice, and the second copy is where the mistakes come from. The fix is not a bigger spreadsheet. It is one place where an order is entered once and everyone sees the same stock, status and delivery. Before you buy or build anything, spend 15 minutes mapping how one order moves through your business. That map tells you what you need.

What the retyping costs you

Most owners guess low, so do the sum with your own numbers:

Question Your number
Orders per day (WhatsApp, phone, walk-in, marketplace)
Minutes per order spent retyping, checking stock or updating someone
Days you take orders per month
Hours per month = orders × minutes × days ÷ 60
Cost per month = hours × your staff's hourly cost (RM)

For example: 10 orders a day at 3 minutes each, 30 days a month, is 900 minutes. That is 15 hours a month of typing the same information a second time. At 30 orders a day it is 45 hours, more than a full working week.

The cost you do not see: mistakes

The hours are the easy part to measure. The expensive part is what goes wrong when the same order is typed twice:

  • Wrong quantity. "2" becomes "20", or the wrong size goes in, because someone copied it from a busy chat.
  • Missed order. A message gets buried under twenty others and never reaches the sheet. The customer finds out first.
  • "Which file is the latest?" orders_final_v7.xlsx and orders_final_v8.xlsx, two people working from two versions of the truth.

Count last month's mistakes: refunds, re-deliveries, apologies, angry reviews. That number usually matters more than the hours.

You are not the only one

A survey of 252 Malaysian SME professionals by the software company Kintone, reported by The Borneo Post in January 2026, found that about one-third of Malaysian SMEs depend heavily on spreadsheets, several separate apps and chat groups for their core work. The problem it describes is the one above: information scattered across places, people duplicating work, and figures that do not match. Kintone sells software, so read the survey with that in mind, but anyone who has run a busy shop on WhatsApp will recognise it.

Map one order in 15 minutes

Take one real order from last week and follow it from the first message to "delivered". Write down every place it was typed, checked or asked about.

Step Who does it Where (app, file, paper) Typed again? Problems?
Customer message
Someone reads it
Written down
Stock check
Payment
Delivery or pickup
"Done"

Then circle three things:

  1. Every step where the same information is typed twice. A system removes these first.
  2. Every step where someone has to ask ("did we deliver?", "do we have stock?"). These need a shared status that everyone can see.
  3. Every step only one person knows how to do. These break when that person is on leave.

If you circled nothing, keep your spreadsheet. If you circled five things, you have found your project.

The six things every order system needs

Whether you buy an app or have one built, check it does these:

  1. One place orders go in. A simple form for staff, or for customers, so an order is typed once instead of copied from chat.
  2. A status everyone sees. New, confirmed, packed, delivered. Nobody has to ask.
  3. Stock that updates itself. Confirming an order takes it off stock, and low stock gets flagged.
  4. A delivery or pickup schedule. Today's and tomorrow's orders on one screen.
  5. Roles. Staff see what they need. The owner sees everything, including sales.
  6. Reports without retyping. Daily sales, best sellers and unpaid orders, exportable for your accountant.

When Excel is still fine

A system is not always the answer. Stay with the spreadsheet if all of these are true:

  • One person handles every order, and there are only a few a day.
  • Nobody else needs to check stock or status while that person is busy.
  • Mistakes are rare and cheap to fix.

If two or more of these stopped being true, you have outgrown the spreadsheet.

Sketch it with Claude Code

You can turn your map into a rough plan and a clickable prototype before talking to anyone. Claude Code is Anthropic's coding assistant. Paste these one at a time in an empty folder, and use made-up sample data, never real customer details.

1. Turn your map into a plan

Here is how one order moves through my business today: [paste your map]. Point out every step where information is typed twice or someone has to ask for a status. Then propose the simplest order system that removes those steps, as a short plan: screens, data fields and who uses each screen. Ask me questions before you assume anything.

2. Build a clickable prototype

Build a simple web prototype of that plan with sample data only: an order form, an orders list with statuses (new, confirmed, packed, delivered), a stock page that goes down when an order is confirmed, and a "today's deliveries" view. Keep it mobile-friendly. Explain how to run it on my computer.

3. Test it like a busy day

Create 30 realistic sample orders for a small [your type of business] in Johor Bahru, including two with mistakes and one cancelled. Walk me through what each screen shows, and list anything my staff would still have to do by hand.

4. Decide what a real version needs

Based on this prototype, list what a production version would need: logins and roles, backups, security, payments, e-invoicing if my sales are above the LHDN threshold, and hosting. Give rough effort for each. What could I keep in a spreadsheet instead?

A prototype is for learning what you need. Before real customer data goes in, it needs logins, backups and proper hosting.

Where to start on Monday

  • Today: do the sum above with your real numbers.
  • This week: map one order with the person who handles them. They will know steps you do not.
  • Next week: circle the retyping and the "did we…?" questions, and decide whether a ready-made app covers them or you need something built around how you work.

Questions people ask

Do I need a custom system, or will an app do?

If a ready-made order or inventory app fits how your team already works, use it: it is cheaper and faster. Custom makes sense when the app forces your team to work around it, or when you need it to connect to things it cannot, such as your own stock rules or accounting.

Can customers still order on WhatsApp?

Yes. Customers can keep messaging you. The difference is that the order is entered once, into the system, instead of being copied into a sheet. Some businesses also send customers an order form link, which removes the typing completely.

How much does an order system cost?

It depends on what it has to do: how many screens, how many users and roles, and what it connects to. Our guide to what a website or system costs in Malaysia explains the ranges. We give a fixed price once we have seen your order map.

Is our data safe in a system?

A proper build has a login for each staff member, roles that limit what each person sees, and regular backups. That is usually safer than a spreadsheet that gets emailed around and copied onto personal phones.
